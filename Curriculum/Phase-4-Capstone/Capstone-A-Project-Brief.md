# Capstone-A: Project Brief

## Arduino Mega Capstone Science Project
### Phase 4 — Weeks 15–18

---

> **Your mission, if you choose to accept it** (and you do — it's graded):
> Design, build, test, and present a device that uses your Arduino Mega to **investigate a science question**, **collect real data**, and **demonstrate a science concept** you care about.

---

## The Mission

You've spent 14 weeks learning to wire circuits, write code, and read sensors. You can blink an LED, measure temperature, detect distance, drive a servo, and display data on an LCD. Now it's time to put all of that together and do something with it.

Your capstone project is a **science investigation device** — not just a gadget, but a tool that:
1. Asks a real science question
2. Collects measurable data to answer it
3. Demonstrates a science concept from the Grade 8 curriculum
4. Follows the scientific method from start to finish
5. Gets presented to the class on Week 18

Think of yourself as a scientist who is also an engineer. A scientist asks "Does ambient light affect plant temperature?" An engineer builds the sensor system to find out. You are doing both.

---

## Project Requirements

Your project **must** satisfy all of the following:

### Hardware Requirements
- [ ] **Must use at least 2 sensors** (e.g., DHT11 + LDR) **OR at least 1 sensor + 1 meaningful feedback output** (e.g., DHT11 + servo vent, LDR + LED grow light, HC-SR04 + buzzer alarm)
- [ ] All components must come from the course kit (ask the teacher before sourcing anything extra)
- [ ] The device must physically work on presentation day — a live demo is expected

### Science Requirements
- [ ] **Must clearly demonstrate or investigate a science concept** from the Grade 8 curriculum (temperature, light, sound, distance, electricity, systems, feedback — pick one and go deep)
- [ ] **Must collect measurable, quantitative data** — not just "it worked" but actual numbers with units
- [ ] **Must apply the scientific method**: you write a hypothesis, run an experiment, record data, and draw a conclusion

### Process Requirements
- [ ] **Planning Template** (`Capstone-C`) must be completed by the end of Session 30
- [ ] **Build Log** (`Capstone-D`) must be completed for all 4 build days (Sessions 31–34)
- [ ] **Final Presentation** (Session 36) — 5 minutes + live demo + Q&A

## Project Constraints

These are the rules of the challenge:

| Constraint | Details |
|------------|---------|
| **Teams** | Groups of 2–3. Roles are shared; everyone contributes to all parts. |
| **Components** | From the course kit only (see inventory card). Ask teacher to approve extras. |
| **Time** | 4 weeks (Sessions 29–36). No extensions beyond Session 36. |
| **Hardware** | Must fit on one breadboard. No soldering required. |
| **Code** | Arduino C++ (.ino sketch). Libraries: DHT.h, Servo.h, LiquidCrystal_I2C.h are all available. |
| **Science** | The project must be testable — "does X affect Y?" not "I think my project is cool." |

## How It's Graded

Your capstone is graded using the **Final Presentation Rubric** (`Capstone-F`). Here's the summary:

| Criterion | Weight | What It Covers |
|-----------|--------|----------------|
| Science Concept | 25% | Is the science question clear? Does the data answer it? Is the conclusion evidence-based? |
| Engineering Design | 25% | Does the circuit work? Are components used correctly? Does the build match the plan? |
| Code Quality | 20% | Is the code clean, commented, and logical? Does it produce useful data? |
| Presentation Skills | 15% | Is the presentation clear, well-structured, and delivered confidently? Does the demo work live? |
| Reflection | 15% | Is the reflection honest, specific, and insightful? (Both the process and the science) |

**Each criterion is marked on a 4-point scale:**
- **4 — Exemplary:** Exceeds expectations. Independent, creative, deep understanding.
- **3 — Proficient:** Meets all expectations. Works correctly with clear understanding.
- **2 — Developing:** Partially meets expectations. Needs some support.
- **1 — Beginning:** Just starting. Needs significant support.

**Note about projects that don't fully work:** A project with a hardware failure on presentation day can still score Proficient in Science Concept, Code Quality, and Reflection. Honest documentation of what went wrong and why is *good science*. Don't give up — document it.

## What Makes a Great Project?

### Must-Have (Proficient level)
- A clear, testable science question
- A hypothesis with all three parts (If… then… because…)
- A working circuit with at least 2 sensors or 1 sensor + 1 feedback output
- Uploaded, working code with comments
- A data table with at least 5 readings per condition
- A conclusion that answers your science question with specific data values
- A 5-minute presentation that all team members contribute to

### What Takes You to Exemplary
- A proportional response (not just on/off — e.g., servo angle proportional to temperature)
- A data graph (line chart, not just a table)
- A conclusion that discusses sources of error
- A reflection that imagines specific next steps with actual component/code ideas
- A live demo that works cleanly and impressively

## Frequently Asked Questions

**"Can we use a project idea from Phase 3?"**
Yes, but your science question and data must be original. Phase 3 was a training exercise. This is your own investigation. Expand on it.

**"What if our project doesn't work on presentation day?"**
Document everything in your Build Log and present your data and analysis honestly. The science process matters as much as the working device. Partial marks are available for every criterion.

**"Can we add components from home?"**
Check with your teacher first. The kit has everything needed for an excellent project. If you want to add something specific (e.g., a soil moisture sensor), ask.

**"Do we have to follow the Idea Bank?"**
No. The Idea Bank (`Capstone-B`) gives you 10 starting ideas, but original ideas are welcome and encouraged. The only rule: your idea must meet the project requirements above.

**"What if my team can't agree?"**
Use the evaluation table in Session 29 to decide objectively. If you're truly stuck, ask the teacher to help mediate. Remember: a simpler idea done well beats an ambitious idea done poorly.

**"Can I work alone?"**
No — teams of 2–3 only. Engineering and science are collaborative by nature, and peer feedback during the build improves the project.

**"Is the code graded?"**
Yes — Code Quality is 20% of the grade. It doesn't need to be perfect, but it needs to work, have meaningful comments, use named constants, and produce clean data.

**"What if we ran out of time and couldn't collect all the data we planned?"**
Collect what you can. Present honestly. In the reflection, describe what data you would have collected with more time and what you would have expected it to show. This demonstrates scientific thinking.

**"Can we use the Tinkercad simulator?"**
For planning and practice, yes! But your final presentation must use the real physical Arduino device.

## One Last Thing

The best capstone projects aren't the ones with the most components or the fanciest code. They're the ones where a team clearly asked a genuine question, genuinely tried to answer it, genuinely learned something — and then explained it to the rest of the class so clearly that everyone understood.

Science is about curiosity and honesty. Bring both.
