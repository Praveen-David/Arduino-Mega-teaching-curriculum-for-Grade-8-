# Week 18, Session 35 — Presentation Prep & Peer Feedback

**Phase:** Phase 4 — Capstone Science Project
**Session Type:** Presentation Preparation / Peer Review

## Learning Objectives
- **Organise** their project findings into a clear, science-focused presentation (poster, slides, or both).
- **Rehearse** a 5-minute presentation that covers the scientific method from question to conclusion.
- **Give** constructive, specific feedback to a peer team using structured prompts.
- **Receive** peer feedback and identify at least two improvements to make before Session 36.

**Science Curriculum Link:** Scientific Communication — Science is incomplete until it is communicated. Scientists publish papers, present at conferences, and explain findings to the public. Students practice this core science skill, learning that a clear explanation of method and evidence is as important as the results themselves.

## 2. Teacher Guide (45-Minute Breakdown)

### 0–5 min — Hook / Warm-Up

**Ask:**
> "Imagine you spent 4 weeks building something amazing and then you had to explain it to your grandmother in 60 seconds. What would you say? What would you leave out?"

Take 3 quick answers. Point out: students instinctively know to focus on the interesting result and the "so what?" — not the technical details.

**Bridge (say this):**
> "Great presentations work the same way. Lead with the interesting question and the interesting answer. Earn your audience's attention before you show the technical details. Today, you turn your science project into a story."

**Hand out `Capstone-F-Final-Presentation-Rubric.md`.**
Walk through the five criteria (Science Concept, Engineering Design, Code Quality, Presentation Skills, Reflection) in 90 seconds. Ask: "By tomorrow, which criterion do you feel strongest about? Which needs the most work?"

---

### 5–15 min — Direct Instruction: What Makes a Great Science Presentation

**Write the 5-slide/5-section structure on the board:**

| Section | Content | Time |
|---------|---------|------|
| 1. Hook & Question | Start with the science question and why it matters (real-world connection) | 30 sec |
| 2. Our Device | How it works — components, circuit overview, what the code does | 60 sec |
| 3. Our Experiment | Hypothesis, method, what we measured and how | 60 sec |
| 4. Data & Results | Data table or graph; what the numbers show | 90 sec |
| 5. Conclusion & Reflection | Does the data support the hypothesis? What we'd do next. What we learned. | 60 sec |

**Total: ~5 minutes + Q&A**

**Say this (the "story arc" of a presentation):**
> "The best science presentations have a story arc: 'We noticed a question → we built a device to test it → here's what we found out → here's what it means.' Every slide should push the story forward."

**Three common presentation mistakes to avoid (write on board):**
1. **Reading from the screen** — look at the audience, not your slides.
2. **Showing all the code** — one screenshot of a key function max; explain what the code *does*, not every line.
3. **Skipping the "so what?"** — always end by saying what your results mean for the science question.

**Demo a 60-second "elevator pitch" for the exemplar project** (or any project you're familiar with):
> "We asked: can a low-cost device monitor greenhouse conditions automatically? We built one using a DHT11 sensor and a servo vent. When temperature rose above 28°C, our device opened a vent automatically. Our data showed the device held temperature within 2°C of our target 80% of the time. This proves that even a simple microcontroller can act as an effective climate controller."

Point out: Science question → device → data → conclusion. One minute.

---

### 15–35 min — Hands-On: Presentation Build + Rehearsal

**Part A: Build (15 min)**

Teams create their presentation. Options:
- **Digital slides** (recommended): 5 slides minimum, 8 maximum
- **Physical poster**: A1 or A0 size with hand-drawn or printed diagrams + data table
- **Both** (for ambitious teams): poster for display + slides for the presentation talk

**Non-negotiable content for every presentation:**
- [ ] Team name and project title
- [ ] Science question and hypothesis (exact text from Planning Template)
- [ ] Circuit overview diagram OR labelled photo of the built circuit
- [ ] At least one screenshot of the Serial Monitor showing real data
- [ ] Data table with actual numbers from Session 34
- [ ] A conclusion that directly answers the science question
- [ ] A reflection: what worked, what didn't, what you'd change

**Optional extras (add if time allows):**
- Graph of data over time (line chart from copied Serial Monitor data)
- Photo of the team building or testing the device
- A "real world application" slide — where could this technology be used?

**Teacher circulates.** Check each team's first slide. Is the science question front and centre? If a team opens with the circuit diagram or a list of components, redirect: "Start with the question — make the audience care."

---

**Part B: Rehearsal + Peer Feedback (20 min)**

**Gallery Walk format (3 stages, ~6–7 minutes each):**

**Stage 1 — Set up displays (2 min)**
Each team arranges their device + poster/screen at their workstation. Optionally, power on the device so it's running during feedback.

**Stage 2 — Two visiting teams give feedback (12 min)**
Each team sends one member to stay at their station and present while one member visits another team.

- Presenter: give your 5-minute talk to the visiting team.
- Visitor: fill in the `Capstone-E-Peer-Feedback-Form.md` while listening.
- Rotate after 6 minutes so each team presents to and hears from at least one other team.

**Stage 3 — Read and act on feedback (6 min)**
Teams return to their station, read the feedback forms, and identify:
- At least 1 "star" (strength to keep)
- At least 1 "wish" (specific improvement for Session 36)

---

### 35–42 min — Testing & Debugging (Presentation Dry Run)

**Final preparation run:**

Each team does a full timed run of their presentation to their own partner.

**Checklist for the timed run:**
- [ ] Did it take 4–6 minutes? (Under 4 = add more detail; over 6 = cut something)
- [ ] Did you say the science question out loud in the first 30 seconds?
- [ ] Did you show your data and explain what it means?
- [ ] Did you explain whether the hypothesis was supported?
- [ ] Did you maintain eye contact with the audience (not just the screen)?
- [ ] Does the device actually turn on and do something visible during the presentation?

**Teacher checks:** walk the room during the dry runs. Note teams who are:
- Over time (help them identify what to cut)
- Under time (prompt: "what else could you explain — what was interesting about the data?")
- Missing the conclusion (most common gap)

//     (shows "Ready" or the project name before readings begin)
// [ ] All thresholds are correctly set from your experiments
// [ ] Code is fully commented — a stranger could understand it
// =============================================================

// PRESENTATION DISPLAY IMPROVEMENT:
// Show a scrolling status message on row 1 of the LCD
// to make the device look polished during the presentation:

// Add this to your global variables:
unsigned long lastScrollTime = 0;    // time of last scroll update
int scrollPos = 0;                   // current scroll position
String projectName = "Smart Greenhouse Monitor";  // change to your name

// Add this function:
void scrollProjectName() {
  // Only scroll every 400 ms
  if (millis() - lastScrollTime > 400) {
    lcd.setCursor(0, 1);
    // Show a 16-character window of the project name
    String display = projectName.substring(scrollPos, scrollPos + 16);
    while (display.length() < 16) display += " ";   // pad to 16 chars
    lcd.print(display);
    scrollPos++;
    if (scrollPos > (int)projectName.length()) scrollPos = 0;
    lastScrollTime = millis();
  }
}
// Call scrollProjectName() from loop() to show the project name
// scrolling across the bottom row of the LCD.

// NOTE: Only add this if you have space in your loop() —
// do not let it slow down your sensor reading frequency.

## 6. Safety Notes

- **Device handling during gallery walk:** Multiple teams moving around the room creates a risk of tripping on USB cables or knocking over devices. Ensure USB cables are tidy and devices are stable before the gallery walk begins.
- **Powered devices unattended:** During the gallery walk, at least one team member should remain at their station at all times while the device is powered.
- **Screen time:** This is an intense screen-focused session. Remind students to look away from screens for 20 seconds every 20 minutes (20-20-20 rule) to reduce eye strain.
- **Printing:** If teams print photos or slides, be aware of ink/printer fumes in poorly-ventilated rooms — brief ventilation breaks are good practice.

| Situation | Action |
|-----------|--------|
| Device stops working during gallery walk | Do not panic; unplug, check wiring, reconnect — this is fixable tonight if needed |
| Peer feedback is unkind or unhelpful | Teacher reminds class: "Specific, kind, helpful" — model a better version of the feedback |
| A team's presentation is significantly under-developed | Teacher sits with them for 5 minutes to scaffold the structure — help them find what they DO have to present |

## 8. Differentiation

### Support
- Provide a **presentation slide template** (pre-formatted in Google Slides or PowerPoint) with placeholders for each section — teams only need to fill in their specific content.
- Allow struggling teams to present in a **"poster only" format** without digital slides — the key criteria are about scientific communication, not presentation software skills.
- Pair a student who is anxious about presenting with a specific, clear role: "Your job is to hold the device and press the button when [specific event] happens." Having a concrete physical task reduces presentation anxiety.
- Provide **sentence frames for the conclusion slide:**
  - "Our data showed that when [independent variable], [dependent variable] [increased/decreased/stayed the same]."
  - "This [supports/refutes] our hypothesis because..."
  - "If we could do this again, we would..."

### Extension
- Challenge the team to prepare for **"hard questions"** from the audience by writing 3 possible tough questions and their answers:
  - "How do you know the DHT11 is accurate enough for your conclusions?"
  - "What would happen if two conditions occurred simultaneously?"
  - "How could you make this device more reliable?"
- Ask the team to prepare a **"technical deep dive" slide** that they keep hidden — only to show if someone in the audience asks a detailed technical question. This rewards preparation and depth.

### Visual / Kinesthetic Accommodations
- For students with presentation anxiety: allow them to present sitting down, or to present to a smaller group of 4–6 students rather than the whole class.
- Allow note cards (3×5 index cards with bullet points) during the presentation — this reduces the cognitive load of memorising and allows students to focus on communication.
- For students who struggle with slide design: allow a purely physical display — the circuit itself, a handwritten poster, and a verbal explanation. The science is what matters.
- Provide a visual **"presentation skeleton"** poster on the wall showing each section as a box with its key content — students can physically point to the poster while building their own presentation.
