# Week 15, Session 29 — Capstone Kickoff & Brainstorming

**Phase:** Phase 4 — Capstone Science Project
**Session Type:** Planning / Ideation (no build, no circuit power-on today)

## Learning Objectives
- **Identify** the requirements of the capstone project brief and explain what makes a project meet the criteria.
- **Connect** prior learning (sensors, code, feedback loops) to potential real-world investigation ideas.
- **Generate** at least three original project ideas as a team using a structured brainstorm process.
- **Select** one project idea and articulate why it satisfies the science and engineering criteria.

**Science Curriculum Link:** Scientific Method & Integrated Science — Students apply the full scientific method to an engineering design challenge, recognizing that real scientists ask questions, design systems, collect evidence, and communicate findings. This session launches that cycle.

## 2. Teacher Guide (45-Minute Breakdown)

### 0–5 min — Hook / Warm-Up

**Display on projector:** A photo collage of four Arduino projects — a weather station displaying data on an LCD, a plant-watering system, a parking sensor with LED indicators, and a theremin-style sound instrument.

**Ask the class:**
> "These were all built with the exact same components you have in your kit. Which one is the most interesting to you — and what *science question* could you investigate with it?"

Give students 60 seconds to think, then take 4–5 quick hands. Don't evaluate answers yet — just build excitement.

**Bridge statement (say this):**
> "Over the next four weeks you are going to design, build, test, and present your own version of something like this. Today we figure out what that 'something' will be. Your kit. Your question. Your science."

---

### 5–15 min — Direct Instruction: The Project Brief & Criteria

**Hand out / display `Capstone-A-Project-Brief.md`.**

Walk through each section out loud. Use this script as a guide:

**Mission:** "Your job is to design a device that *demonstrates or investigates a science concept* — not just a gadget that does a cool trick. The science question is the heart of it."

**Key requirements (write these on the board):**
1. Must use **≥ 2 sensors** OR **1 sensor + 1 feedback output** (servo, buzzer, LEDs, LCD)
2. Must clearly **demonstrate or test a science concept** from the Grade 8 curriculum
3. Must **collect measurable data** (numbers — temperature readings, distances, time intervals, etc.)
4. Must **apply the scientific method**: question → hypothesis → experiment → results → conclusion
5. Must include a **Build Log** (daily) and a **Final Presentation**

**Concrete analogy (say this):**
> "Think of yourself as a scientist who also happens to be an engineer. A scientist asks 'Does shade affect plant temperature?' An engineer builds the thermometer and the shade controller. You're doing both at the same time — that's what makes this exciting."

**Show the Idea Bank:** Walk through 3 or 4 examples briefly from `Capstone-B-Project-Idea-Bank.md`. For each, identify: *What is the science question? What sensors are used? What data would you collect?*

Clarify constraints:
- Work in **teams of 2–3** (teacher assigns or students choose — your call)
- You must use components **from the course kit** (no purchasing extras without teacher approval)
- The device must **physically work** on presentation day — partial credit exists but a live demo is the goal

**Take questions.** Common ones and suggested answers:

| Student Question | Suggested Answer |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| "Can we do a project we already saw in Phase 3?" | "You can use it as inspiration, but your *science question* and *data* must be original. Expand on it." |
| "What if our idea doesn't work?" | "Debugging *is* science. Document what went wrong and why — that's still full credit on the process." |
| "Can we add components from home?" | "Check with me first. Kit components are enough for an excellent project." |
| "Do we have to do exactly what's in the Idea Bank?" | "No — the Idea Bank is a starting point. Original ideas are welcome and encouraged." |

---

### 15–35 min — Hands-On: Team Formation & Brainstorm

**Step 1 — Form teams (5 min)**
Announce team assignments (or allow self-selection — your call). Each team needs a team name and roles:
- **Project Manager** — keeps the team on task, tracks the timeline
- **Chief Builder** — leads circuit construction
- **Lead Coder** — leads the code
- **Science Officer** — tracks the hypothesis, data, and science connection

(In teams of 2 share roles; everyone does everything but someone is "lead" on each area.)

**Step 2 — Silent Brainstorm with sticky notes (7 min)**
Each person writes **one idea per sticky note** — aim for at least 4 per person in 7 minutes. No judging yet. Prompts written on the board:
- "What in everyday life would you want to *measure or monitor*?"
- "What science topic from this year were you most curious about?"
- "What problem at home or school could a sensor solve?"
- "Which session from Phases 1–3 was your favourite to build?"

**Step 3 — Share & cluster (5 min)**
Team members read their ideas aloud and stick them on the team's large paper. Group similar ideas together. Circle any that:
- Uses sensors from the kit ✔
- Has a clear science question ✔
- Could produce data ✔

**Step 4 — Narrow to top 3 (5 min)**
Each team circles their top 3 ideas. For each, ask:
- "What is the science question?"
- "What would we measure?"
- "Which components do we need?"

Write quick answers next to each idea.

**Step 5 — Choose 1 (3 min)**
Teams vote or reach consensus. **Circle the winner.**

Teacher circulates during this time. Check in with every team. Prompt stuck teams with questions from the Idea Bank.

---

### 35–42 min — Testing & Debugging (Idea Validation)

This session has no circuit — "testing" here means **validating the idea** before teams commit.

Each team briefly shares their chosen idea with the class (30–60 seconds each). After each share, the class gives a quick **"Thumbs: Up / Side / Down"** on:
1. Does it clearly have a science question?
2. Does it use sensors/outputs from the kit?
3. Does it seem achievable in 4 weeks?

Teacher gives brief honest feedback. If an idea is too vague, too simple, or impossible with the kit, redirect now — it's much easier to pivot in Session 29 than in Session 32.

**What success looks like at this point:**
- Every team has chosen a project idea with a clear science question.
- They can name at least 2 components they will use.
- They are enthusiastic and beginning to think about "how."

## 6. Safety Notes

**Today's session has no electronics powered on.** However, use this session to reinforce safe habits for the build weeks ahead:

- **Remind teams:** The mantra is **Build → Check → Power on** — never the other way.
- **Component handling:** If teams look in kit boxes, they should not remove components without purpose. Leads on LEDs and resistors are sharp — handle carefully.
- **Workspace:** Keep food, drink, and clutter away from the kit area even when not building. Good habits start now.
- **Ergonomics:** Build weeks involve sitting at workstations for extended periods — encourage students to sit up straight and take micro-breaks between tasks.

**General "if something goes wrong" for this session:**
| Situation | Action |
|-----------|--------|
| Team cannot agree on a project | Teacher mediates; use the evaluation table to decide objectively |
| A proposed idea is impossible with kit components | Teacher redirects to Idea Bank alternatives in Session 30 |
| A student is disengaged or not contributing | Assign a specific role with a concrete task (e.g., "your job is to fill in the evaluation table") |

## 8. Differentiation

### Support
- Provide a **pre-filled evaluation table** with the Idea Bank's top 4 ideas already entered — students just circle their choice and explain why.
- Assign the "Science Officer" role to a student who struggles with hardware but is strong in science literacy — this gives them an important, confidence-building job.
- Offer a **sentence stem** for the science connection: "Our project is like real-world [technology/research] because it also [measures/controls/responds to] ________________."
- Allow teams to choose directly from the Idea Bank rather than generating original ideas.

### Extension
- Challenge the team to identify **two possible hypotheses** for their project (one they think will be confirmed, one they think will be surprising) and explain the difference.
- Ask them to sketch a **system diagram** (inputs → process → outputs) for their project before Session 30.
- Research a related **Arduino library** they will need and write a one-paragraph summary of what it does.

### Visual / Kinesthetic Accommodations
- Allow students to physically hold components from the kit while discussing project ideas ("What can this sensor detect? What could that control?").
- Post a large visual "component menu" on the wall — photos of each component with labels.
- Provide a printed Idea Bank with **pictures** for each project (teacher can Google images and add to a printed version).
- For students with writing difficulties, allow the warm-up questions to be answered verbally to a partner who scribes.
